What does free options data cost?

Anyone without a research budget prices options from free, indicative quotes. This project measures how far those quotes land from Cboe's published volatility indices, and finds that most of the gap was never the data.

The gap that looked like bad data

On 4 September, the project's at-the-money readings sat below Cboe's published index for every matched pair, by 3.61 volatility points on average. The obvious explanation was the feed. Alpaca's free options data is indicative rather than consolidated OPRA, and its own staff describe it as meant for debugging code, not testing strategies.

The obvious explanation was premature. An at-the-money quote is a single point on the volatility smile, while Cboe's indices integrate every strike. Rebuilding Cboe's own model-free method on the same free feed, across a wide enough band of strikes, closes almost all of the gap. SPY came in at 15.83 against a published VIX of 15.84.

Where the gap came from

The error was strike truncation. Cboe's formula sums out-of-the-money option prices across every strike until quotes run out, and the tails carry real weight. Stop the sum at a fixed percentage of spot and whatever lies beyond it is lost. How much is lost depends on how wide the distribution is, which is to say on volatility.

A ±10% band covers 2.2 standard deviations of a 16-volatility index and only 0.6 of 59-volatility crude oil. That is why USO read twelve points light. The lognormal model in Figure 1 predicts nearly the same shortfall with no market data at all: 11.43 points against 12.37 measured.

The model has no skew, so it should understate the real shortfall, and it does at every band. The practical consequence is uncomfortable: any study using a fixed percentage strike band is most biased on exactly the high-volatility names most likely to be interesting.

Band in standard deviations is the half-width of the band divided by σ√T, with T of 30 days.
At ±20% and ±10%, only the mean and the worst of the five underlyings were recorded, not each one.

Why a month of daily rows is not a month of evidence

The volatility risk premium compares what options implied with what the market then did. With 30-day windows, consecutive daily observations share almost the same future, so a month of rows is closer to one observation than to twenty.

The project's simulator makes the cost concrete. Give it a premium that is zero by construction, and a test that treats every day as independent reports a significant premium 63.8% of the time, or 93.0% once a common market factor is added. A test on non-overlapping windows stays near its nominal 5%.

That is why the live panel on this page reports collection and never a premium. The level of the premium is reported only from the long Cboe sample, which carries about 127 non-overlapping episodes per pair.

Run it yourself with python analyze.py --simulate.
Market factor means a shared shock with correlation 0.5 across names, the case that matters for a universe of related tickers.

The premium, where it can be measured

Before trusting any estimator on a short sample, the project pointed it at a decade of Cboe index data. The premium is a documented phenomenon, so this was a test of the code rather than a discovery. If the estimators could not find it there, nothing they reported about a few weeks of collection would be worth reading.

They found it on 9 of 11 pairs. The two that fail are sector indices Cboe discontinued in February 2022, which have the fewest observations. Measured in log variance rather than volatility, the same premium is stronger still, with t = 16.26 against 9.28. That result is registered but not adopted.

Intervals are approximate: the mean plus or minus 1.96 standard errors, with the standard error recovered from the reported t-statistic.
Premium is implied minus realised volatility, in annualised points. Positive means the insurance was overpriced.

Written down before the data arrived

Every hypothesis is committed to the repository before the data that tests it is examined, and the commit date is the evidence. Post-hoc adjustments are logged in the same file, and a third adjustment means abandoning the hypothesis.

What this cannot show

  • One day is calibration. The model-free comparison has been run on a single day. H3 is tested on the series that begins collecting on 14 September 2026.
  • The feed is indicative. Alpaca's free options data is not consolidated OPRA. Levels are approximate, and changes over time are more trustworthy than any single reading.
  • The daily panel is short. It carries roughly two independent episodes so far, so it reports collection, not a premium.
  • H4 is close to a replication. Its predictions come from published work. What is new is the data: collected first-hand, at zero budget, with volume and open interest per contract.
  • No licensed data is redistributed. Nothing on this site is derived from Bloomberg, and no Cboe or FRED series is mirrored. Index levels are quoted only where a result depends on them.
