implied volatility, cross-section

Every dot is one ticker: what the options market expects it to move over the coming month, read from the at-the-money call about 30 days out. The range is the point. A universe of correlated tech names would be one observation repeated many times. Hover a dot to identify it, click to pin it, and step through the trading days to watch the cross-section move.

term structure

Near-expiry implied volatility against the far expiry for the same name. Above the diagonal the market charges more for the longer horizon, which is the usual shape. Below it the curve is inverted, and near-term risk is priced above longer-term risk.

put against call

Put minus call implied volatility at the same strike, in volatility points, for the names furthest from parity in each direction. At the money the two should nearly agree, so a large gap often says more about quote quality than about sentiment.

quote quality

A wide bid-ask spread makes the mid price unreliable, so this decides which tickers stay. Weekend readings run 1.5 to 14 times wider than intraday: on a Saturday even PEP, a mega-cap staple, reads 98%. Only a trading-day row settles it.

the universe

what this is measuring

Implied volatility is the market's forecast. Realised volatility is what actually happened afterwards. The gap between them, the volatility risk premium, is generally positive, because option sellers charge for bearing risk. This project tests whether that holds on data collected first-hand rather than bought.

One snapshot a day per ticker: the at-the-money call about 30 days out, the put at the same strike, and a second, further expiry. Nothing here can be reconstructed later, which is why the recorder matters more than the analysis.

The strike surface adds every strike within 30% of spot for eight underlyings, with volume and open interest per contract. It is what lets a free-data estimate be checked against Cboe's published index, which is the question the paper asks.
